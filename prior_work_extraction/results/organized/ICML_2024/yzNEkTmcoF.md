## Synthesis

First, Athey and Imbens (2006) established the CiC framework, showing how monotone rank mappings can nonparametrically identify entire counterfactual outcome distributions in a DID setting. This provides the conceptual and technical backbone for moving beyond mean effects under parallel trends toward distributional identification. Second, Gruber (1994) popularized the triple-difference design as a practical way to relax DID’s parallel trends assumption by using an additional, unaffected margin. Meyer (1995) further codified DDD within the design-based causal inference toolkit, motivating its use when conventional DID credibility is strained.

Meanwhile, Bitler, Gelbach, and Hoynes (2006) demonstrated empirically that policy evaluations focused on averages can miss meaningful heterogeneity, providing direct impetus to develop distributional DDD methods that recover full counterfactual distributions. Together, these works directly shape the Triple Changes estimator’s identification strategy and its motivation to deliver distributional policy effects where traditional DID/DDD fall short.
